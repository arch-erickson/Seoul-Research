Seoul: The Carrot and the Stick

20 content slides · 18 photo pairs · 38:30 talk · 5 minutes of questions

Seoul and New York face the same problem, because buildings that already stand cause about two-thirds of the emissions in each city. For those buildings, Seoul mostly uses carrots, like cheap city retrofit loans and rewards for saving energy, while New York mostly uses a stick, the limits and fines of Local Law 97. Seoul's record, including its 2026 landfill trouble, suggests New York should keep its fines and add city loans so owners can pay for the work.

Five things to remember

  1. Same problem in both cities: buildings that already stand cause about two-thirds of emissions (Seoul 67%, New York 66 to 71%).
  2. Seoul lends and New York fines. From 2013 Seoul's BRP lent up to 100% of the eligible cost at 1.75% a year, and LL97 charges $268 for each ton over the limit on buildings over 25,000 sq ft.
  3. Seoul's rewards worked at scale. One Less Nuclear Power Plant reached 2.04 million TOE in June 2014, six months early, and Eco-Mileage had 1.68 million participants.
  4. Seoul's landfill ban started January 1, 2026, before the region had enough incinerators, so some trash now travels about 130 km by road to other cities.
  5. The five ideas are food waste charged by weight, city retrofit loans, balcony solar kits, a transit pass named for the climate and highway removal. The retrofit loans are the main one, because about 63% of LL97 buildings would miss the 2030 limits if nothing changes.

Background

The title comes from an old saying about getting a donkey to move. You can hold a carrot in front of it as a reward, or tap it with a stick as a punishment. In city climate policy, a carrot is something like a cheap loan, a subsidy or reward points. A stick is something like a fine or a ban. The talk looks at how Seoul and New York use these two kinds of tools on the same problem, which is getting existing buildings to pollute less.

Seoul is a fair match for New York. The city of Seoul has about 9.4 million people and New York City about 8.3 million. Both cities are very dense and build tall, and in both, buildings cause roughly two-thirds of the greenhouse gas emissions. Neither city has empty land left for big new parks. Because they face the same limits, an idea that works in Seoul has a real chance of working in New York.

You open with composting because everyone can picture it. Seoul uses shared smart bins for food waste. A resident taps a card, the bin weighs what goes in, and that household is billed by weight. New York made curbside composting mandatory, but in 2025 organics were only about 2.4% of the residential waste the city collected. Most people in the room probably did not compost last night, so the question opens the comparison in a friendly way.

You also tell the class early that one Seoul policy is in trouble right now. Since January 1, 2026, Seoul, Incheon and Gyeonggi can no longer send household trash straight to a landfill. The region did not build enough incinerators in time, so some trash is now sent to other regions. Slide 15 covers this. Saying it on the first slide shows the class you will include Seoul's problems along with its successes.

The five ideas come on slide 19: food waste charged by weight, city loans for building retrofits, balcony solar kits, a transit pass named for the climate, and removing a highway the way Seoul did at Cheonggyecheon. You only need to promise them here.

Likely questions

Why compare New York with Seoul? Why not a city in Europe?

Seoul matches New York on the hard parts. The city populations are close (about 9.4 million and 8.3 million), both cities are dense and tall, and buildings cause roughly two-thirds of emissions in both. Neither has open land for big new parks, so lessons move between them more easily.

What exactly is the carrot and what is the stick?

For existing buildings, Seoul's main tool is a carrot. Its Building Retrofit Program lent owners cheap city money for upgrades, and from 2013 it lent up to 100% of the eligible cost at 1.75% interest. New York's main tool is a stick. Local Law 97 fines owners $268 for every ton of CO₂e their building emits over its limit.

Doesn't Seoul use sticks too?

Yes. Korea has made people buy priced trash bags since 1995, and Seoul's smart bins bill food waste by weight. Under Korea's national rules, many new buildings must meet a zero-energy grade. The most polluting diesel vehicles (Grade 5) are banned from central Seoul, and the 2026 landfill ban is a rule with no reward attached. The title describes the main tool each city uses on buildings that already exist. For those, Seoul leans on loans and rewards and New York leans on fines.

Is composting actually required in New York?

Yes, curbside composting is mandatory. In 2025, organics were only about 2.4% of residential waste collected, up from 1.4% in 2024. Fines were set to start in April 2025, were paused within three weeks, and came back on January 1, 2026, with 330 summonses in the first stretch.

Which Seoul policy is struggling?

The ban on sending household trash straight to landfills, which started January 1, 2026. It was passed in 2021 with five years to prepare, but the region did not add enough incinerators. Seoul has only four, and most are 20 to 30 or more years old, so some districts now send trash to other regions. Slide 15 covers it.

Background

The two cities are close in size. Seoul has about 9.4 million people in the city and about 26 million in the metro area. New York has about 8.3 million in the city and about 20 million in the metro area. All four numbers are rounded. Both cities are very dense and build tall, and both rely heavily on transit (New York's use is very high by US standards). Seoul's housing is mostly apartments, and New York has a mix of building types. Neither city has free land for big new parks.

Buildings are the biggest source of emissions in both cities. Seoul's current climate plan puts buildings at 67% of the city's greenhouse gas emissions. New York's share changes from one emissions inventory to the next, between 66 and 71%, so the slide shows a range. In both cities, about two-thirds of the problem comes from the buildings themselves.

Seoul's main tool for older buildings is the Building Retrofit Program (BRP), which started in 2008. Under it, the city lent owners money to upgrade their buildings so they would use less energy. It was extended to private buildings such as universities and hospitals, then to homes in 2012, when it grew under One Less Nuclear Power Plant. In 2013 Seoul cut the interest rate from 2.5% to 1.75% a year and raised the most it would lend from 80% to 100% of the eligible cost. After that, an owner could borrow up to the full eligible cost from the city at a low rate and pay it back over time. Your sources don't say whether these exact terms still apply. Seoul's current climate plan talks about long-term interest-free loans for upgrades to existing buildings.

New York's main tool is Local Law 97 (LL97), passed in 2019. It covers buildings over 25,000 square feet. Each building gets a yearly carbon limit per square foot based on how it is used, for example offices or apartments. If a building goes over its limit, the owner pays $268 for every ton of CO₂e above it. The law aims for 40% lower emissions by 2030 and 80% lower by 2050, compared with 2005. The limits get much stricter in 2030. About 11% of covered buildings were estimated to be over the 2024 to 2029 limits, and about 63% are projected to be over the 2030 limits if nothing changes.

Put together, this is where the title comes from. Seoul helps owners pay for upgrades, and New York charges owners who stay over the limit. Your sources describe LL97 as leaving the financing to owners. That gap is the reason for your main recommendation on slide 19, a city loan program for retrofits that works alongside LL97.

Background

The Seoul Energy Dream Center opened in 2012 in World Cup Park, in the Mapo-gu district. It is Korea's first zero-energy public building. It is about 3,500 m² and works as a public exhibition and education center about renewable energy. The Seoul city government commissioned it. The design was led by Fraunhofer ISE, a German research institute for solar energy, which ran a German and Korean team that designed the building and oversaw construction.

The site is the old Nanjido landfill from slide 4. Nanjido was an island in the Han River that became Seoul's official dump in 1978. Trash piled up about 90 m high in two mounds, and there was no modern system to treat the gas or the polluted liquid coming out of it. The city decided to close the dump in 1993, and the site reopened as World Cup Park for the 2002 World Cup. Landfill gas is now captured and used. Trash can't carry the weight of a building, so the Energy Dream Center stands on piles that go down through the waste to solid ground, with a reinforced concrete base plate on top. The dossier says only that the building sits on piles on a reinforced concrete base plate. Reaching solid ground is how piles usually work, and the dossier gives no depth for them.

The shape is the first energy strategy. The plan is square. The building widens as it rises over its three floors and turns 45°, with a square roof on top. The envelope, meaning the walls, roof and windows, was designed to Passive House standards, so it is very well insulated and airtight. Geothermal heat pumps handle heating and cooling. It can send extra power out on sunny days and draw power when the panels make less than it needs. It is reported to use over 70% less energy than a similar ordinary building, and it holds Korea's KGBC green building certification and the Building Energy Efficiency Label.

The building connects the landfill story and the zero-energy story. It sits on the landfill Seoul turned into a park, and it shows the zero-energy idea from slide 9 working on a real public building in 2012, years before the national rule for public buildings began in 2020. LEED is a green building rating from the US Green Building Council. It has four levels, Certified, Silver, Gold and Platinum, so Gold is the second highest. Both buildings on this slide were finished in 2017 and both are LEED Gold, which makes them a good pair to compare.

Background

Since 1995, everyone in Korea, Seoul included, has paid for general trash by buying official priced bags. General trash means anything that is not recycling or food waste, which are kept separate. The more trash you throw out, the more bags you have to buy, so people have a reason to throw out less. This kind of system is called pay as you throw, and Korea's version is the one other countries usually point to.

Food waste goes into shared smart bins instead. A resident taps a household RFID card on the bin, the lid opens, the bin weighs what goes in, and that household is billed for the weight. The 3D diagram on the slide walks through these three steps. The 4.2 kg on the bin's display is only an example.

There is a reward too. A household that cuts its food waste by 10 to 30% compared with the same period a year earlier can earn up to 5,000 points in each evaluation period, worth about ₩5,000. The separated food waste is turned into animal feed, compost and biogas.

The results are on the slide. The bag fees cut trash by about 8% in 1995 and 11% by 1996, both compared with 1994. Seoul Solution warns that the 1995 number may be too high, because many people threw out large items for free that year, so the 1996 number is the more reliable one. For food waste, Korean agency reporting from December 2013 gives a 10 to 30% drop, and a comparison table on the same site gives 9 to 31% for the weighing bins.

New York has made curbside composting mandatory, and buildings can be fined $25 to $100 if they have 1 to 8 units, and $100 to $300 if they have 9 or more. But New York's brown bins don't weigh anything and there is no reward, so a household never learns how much food it throws out. Seoul's bin gives each household its own number every time. Background

Nanjido (nahn-jee-doh) was an island in the Han River. Work on an embankment joining it to the mainland began in January 1977, and in 1978 it became Seoul's official dump. It was picked because it was easy to reach and far from downtown.

Over about 15 years the trash piled up into two mounds about 90 m high, roughly as tall as a 30-story building. Korea had no modern system for landfill gas or leachate at the time. Leachate is the dirty liquid that drains out of buried trash. Rotting trash makes methane, and the site gave off methane and toxic gases.

Planning to fix the site started in 1991. In 1993 the city decided to close the dump and stabilize the ground (some sources say dumping ended in 1992). The 3D diagram shows the general idea of the fix: a sealed cap over the trash, wells that collect the gas, and a park on top. The gas is now captured and used. Construction ran from 1996 to 2002, and the site opened as World Cup Park for the 2002 FIFA World Cup. The two mounds became Haneul Park (hah-nool, meaning sky) and Noeul Park (no-ool).

In 2012 the Seoul Energy Dream Center, Korea's first zero-energy public building, opened on the same ground (slide 10). The old trash can't carry a building's weight, so the center stands on piles that reach down to solid ground.

New York is doing the same thing at Freshkills Park on Staten Island. Fresh Kills was once the world's largest landfill, and it is becoming a park of about 2,200 acres that opens in phases. Background

In 2021, a law banned direct landfilling of household waste in Seoul, Incheon and Gyeonggi, the area together called the capital region. Direct landfilling means burying raw household trash. Under the ban, trash has to be burned or processed first. The law gave the region a five-year grace period to get ready.

During those five years, local governments mostly did not add incinerator capacity. One news report says they effectively neglected it. Seoul still runs only four public incinerators, in Gangnam, Mapo, Nowon and Yangcheon, and most are 20 to 30 or more years old. The ban still started on schedule on January 1, 2026, with no emergency delay.

From January to June 2026, Seoul's privately handled household waste reached 457 tons a day, a 3.2-fold rise. Public incineration went up by only 80 tons (from 1,921 to 2,001 tons). As a result, much of the trash went to private companies, and some of it left the region. Gangdong district ships waste to private incinerators in Cheonan and Sejong, and Gangnam district plans to send waste to Cheongju. On the map, the straight-line distances from central Seoul are about 85 km to Cheonan, 110 km to Cheongju and 125 km to Sejong. Seoul Economic Daily reported that some trash travels about 130 km, which would be the distance by road.

The cities receiving the trash are pushing back. Operators in Cheongju agreed not to bid on contracts from Seoul, Incheon or Gyeonggi through the end of next year. This is an environmental justice problem, because the trash ends up in communities that didn't make it. Amendments to the Wastes Control Act are pending in the National Assembly with no clear timing, and the nearby city of Seongnam is building a new 500-ton-a-day incinerator to replace an old one.

The slide ends with a question for the class. New York's Local Law 97 fines buildings that go over their emissions limits, and about 63% of covered buildings would miss their 2030 limits if nothing changes. Seoul set its deadline before the incinerators were ready. The question is whether New York's retrofit workers and supply chain can keep up with what LL97 asks for. Background

Cheonggyecheon (chung-gyeh-chun) is a stream in central Seoul. It was buried under an elevated highway that carried about 168,000 vehicles a day. From 2003 to 2005, Seoul took the highway down and opened the stream back up. Uncovering a buried stream like this is called daylighting. The project cost about US$281 million and restored about 5.8 km (3.6 miles) of corridor.

Wildlife came back. A Landscape Architecture Foundation case study reports that between 2003 and 2008 plant species went from 62 to 308, insects from 15 to 192, birds from 6 to 36 and fish from 4 to 25. You'll often see a "639% increase in biodiversity" quoted. It's an overall index, so it can't be read as a species count. The counts for each group are safer to give. Plants alone went up about 397%, so there were about five times as many plant species.

The air got cleaner and the area got cooler. Small-particle air pollution fell from 74 to 48 micrograms per cubic meter, about 35% less. The highest cooling estimate says the corridor is 3.3 to 5.9°C cooler than a parallel road 4 to 7 blocks away. Other sources find a smaller effect. WWF says 3 to 4°C cooler than areas 400 m away. Metropolis says the area went from 2.2°C above the Seoul average to 1.3°C above. A peer-reviewed study that pulled many studies together is the most careful source, and it found lower daily average temperatures within about 300 m of the stream.

Many people expected traffic chaos when the highway closed, and it didn't happen. In general, when road space is taken away, some drivers take other routes, some switch to transit, and some trips stop happening. Planners call this reduced demand. It's the opposite of induced demand, where new roads fill up with new traffic. Bus ridership in the corridor rose 15.1% from 2003 to 2008, but Seoul's big 2004 bus reform happened at the same time, so the stream shouldn't get all the credit.

For New York, this is evidence for the debates over the Brooklyn-Queens Expressway (BQE), the Cross Bronx Expressway and the Gowanus Expressway. The High Line reused an old rail line, so no drivers lost a road. Cheonggyecheon removed a busy road that drivers used every day, and the gridlock people predicted did not happen.

Background

In July 2004 Seoul rebuilt its whole bus network and switched it over in one night. Before the reform, most subway lines shared a fare system, but buses were left out of it. The reform tied them together with one fare based on distance and free transfers between bus and subway. The same fare system later spread to Gyeonggi Province in July 2007 and to Incheon in 2009.

The reform also changed the streets. Main roads got bus lanes in the middle of the road, with island stops where riders wait between the lanes of traffic. Routes were sorted into four colors: blue for long main routes, green for local neighborhood routes, red for express routes to the suburbs and yellow for downtown loops. The four colors made the network easier to read at a glance.

The results were strong, with a few cautions. Bus speeds rose by up to 11 km/h, and travel speeds on main roads rose 33 to 50% (December 2004). Bus accidents fell 24% in the first year. Research linked to UN-Habitat calls this the reform's most dramatic success. Ridership went up too, but the size depends on which years are compared, from about 9 to 21%. UN-Habitat's cautious figure is about 9%, or 406,000 more riders a day (September 2003 to September 2004). UN-Habitat also found that service got much better, but in the first few years the subsidies didn't come down.

The Climate Card came twenty years later. It was announced in September 2023, piloted in January 2024 and fully launched in July 2024.
